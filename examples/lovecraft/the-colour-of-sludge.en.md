# The Colour of Sludge

I write this because, since the careless publication of the municipal report on the cove of São Bento, three men of office and one provincial doctor have begun repeating an explanation that may kill other curious souls. Their version speaks of saline emanations, collective hysteria, and the deformation of old buoys; mine, which I kept back out of prudence and shame, depends on notes taken before I myself accepted the danger. I ask not for complete belief, but for the suspension of that robust curiosity which sends young engineers down into the mud when the tide withdraws too far. I add that it was not in my temperament to seek notoriety through maritime terrors, and that my education, though limited, sufficed to distinguish magnetic declination from harbour fantasy. I brought with me receipts, dated sketches, and the copy of the dispatch that now compels me to speak. Those papers may survive better than my voice, for since that week I stumble over certain words as though they had lost their natural direction. Such precaution is all the more necessary because the dispatch turns my reserve into complicity, and because no one, reading only administrative figures, will perceive the species of invitation a technical error may conceal.

I was called to the place in March, as a modest draughtsman of plans for a dredging company that intended to open a smaller channel beside the mangrove. São Bento did not appear on tourist maps, having only a quay of greened stone, two lime warehouses, and a closed hermitage whose bell had been removed without parish record. The first abnormal sign seemed ridiculous: all the compasses, including Captain Faria's pocket compass, erred by a few degrees only when pointed toward a strip of grey mud among the roots. The people there possessed the dry reserve of settlements that have seen many fevers and few miracles, so that no one hastened to adorn my work with legends. Only an old woman, keeper of the hermitage keys, refused to cross the square when my instruments were left on the quay table. She said the metal woke an ancient thing; then fell silent, as though the phrase had betrayed her. Even the dogs, which slept beside the barrels, avoided the table whenever the compass lay there, and this domestic detail, of no scientific value, later returned with an insistence that still wears me down. I recorded the detail out of professional habit, though the overseer laughed and attributed everything to rust in the winches.

Later I learned that the fishermen had avoided the strip since the childhood of their grandfathers, not for fear of drowning, since there the water scarcely covered the ankles, but because any stake driven into that stretch reappeared at dawn twisted against the current. Local superstition called it the bottom's breath, a crude name that seemed less foolish to me when I saw the mud move without bubbles. I examined the stake the following morning and found the wood sound, although its fibres opened at angles no lateral force could have produced. The clay stuck to its point had dried in plates so regular they seemed cut by a ruler. I kept one of them out of simple curiosity, and that curiosity, which then seemed to me a virtue, was the first moral failure of the whole affair.

Each time I turned the clay plate over, its faces seemed to exchange orientation without altering form, a phenomenon too small to denounce and too great to forget. On the second afternoon, in the damp archive of the larger warehouse, I found an 1879 notebook belonging to a surveyor named Valério Serrão. Its pages, eaten at the edges, described the same deviation of needles and added drawings of a net of parallel lines beneath the sludge, lines he swore he had measured by means of graduated rods before abandoning them. The final note spoke of neither monster nor apparition, and for that reason disturbed me more: it said only that the lines obeyed a tide that did not come from the sea.

Serrão's name was missing from the official lists; two kerosene receipts and a collection letter, however, proved that he had been there for six weeks. The tone of his notes passed gradually from technical impatience to an almost sick concision, as though every sentence were written against an outward pressure. One drawing, repeated more often than the others, showed nine lines that never touched on the paper and yet were marked by him as a single line seen from the wrong place. I do not know whether Serrão went mad, fled, or simply understood before us the uselessness of remaining where measurement itself began to serve another space. I sought to confirm the notebook by ordinary means, for I still retained enough contempt for quay-side tales. I buried three iron markers, made scale sketches, and asked the telegraph boy to record the exact hour of each withdrawal of the water.

By the third cycle, the markers formed an impossible triangle, not because they moved very far, but because they preserved constant distances among themselves while the entire orientation turned like a leaf caught in an invisible current. I do not expect such measurements to convince anyone who did not stand before them, for common language turns every impossibility into a copyist's trick. I can only state that I remade the marks with different instruments, and that the result mocked them all equally. Faria, who had previously scorned my caution, fell silent when a bottle left among the markers rolled up the slight incline and stopped at the least low point. The telegraph boy, seeing my drawings, asked whether I had used wet paper, for he swore the lines drew closer whenever no one was breathing over the table. It was then I noticed the smell, if smell one may call a dry impression of heated stone inside the throat.

None of the workers perceived it at the same instant as I did, and each, when questioned separately, indicated a different origin: the lime kiln, a candle extinguished in the hermitage, the hull of a boat that no longer existed. This divergence did not weaken my suspicion; rather it suggested that something was using ordinary memories to clothe a presence without any quality of its own. Each man's expression changed according to the memory chosen by the odour, and it was this variation that prevented me from accepting a simple chemical cause. The young telegraph boy went pale speaking of a hospital room; the caulker swore he heard wet firewood; Faria made the sign of the cross without realizing it. None of this came from the mangrove as vapour, but from some fitting between the mangrove and intimate deposits that should not have answered to a tide.

Since then I distrust the senses that offer themselves as memory, for perhaps they are less windows of the soul than instruments available to faceless forces. The following night I agreed to accompany Faria to the uncovered strip, taking lantern, cord, notebook, and a courage so theatrical that today it seems almost obscene to me. The moon made of the mangrove a script of broken shadows, and the mud, which from a distance had seemed smooth, revealed a texture of tiny inclined blades, all turned toward a point without reference in the sky. When the lantern touched that point on the ground, there was no gleam, noise, or opening; there was only the physical certainty that I was looking at the inner side of a surface larger than the earth.

There was, however, a severe order in that appearance of disorder, and perhaps for that reason I continued when all prudence commanded me to withdraw. The blades did not shine; they received the light as numbers receive an operation, changing without visible transition. I felt, before I could formulate it, that we were not before an opening, but before the shallow edge of an operation that only by accident touched our senses. Faria noticed my trembling and smiled with pity, a common gesture that for one second made still more abominable the exact indifference of the mud before us. Faria wanted to retreat, but the cord that bound us to the quay began to weigh as though hanging vertically downward. I remember with shameful clarity that I tried to explain the fact by suction, by magnetism, by any word capable of keeping the scene within learned mechanics.

Then the captain showed me the shadow of our legs cast in the direction opposite to the lantern's light, and in that shadow there was, for a moment, the shape of steps thrown by no body. Faria murmured an incomplete prayer, and the prayer seemed to prolong itself on the ground in small straight breaks. I heard no voice in answer, yet the steps we took backward produced, ahead of us, successive depressions in the untouched mud. It was in that instant that I lost my last confidence in the notion of distance, for every retreat seemed only to deepen our participation in the point from which we fled. None of those depressions corresponded to the size of our feet, and all of them, when touched by the lantern, displayed that same impossible tendency to turn inward. I do not claim to have seen inhabitants or intelligence, and it would be more honest to confess that the word intelligence still seems to me too human.

What I saw was this: a rule at work. The mud-blades changed inclination according to pulses coming from no depth at all, and each change brought into accord the absent bell, Serrão's rods, and the error of the compasses. I understood that the place, instead of hiding a thing beneath the mangrove, exposed a stretch of correspondence between our matter and a system whose natural direction crossed us askew. I say rule for lack of a more decent term, but the word suggests intention where perhaps there was only compatibility. What surrounded us did not hate, call, or hunt; it adjusted. And that adjustment, perceived in a poor cove by two men unworthy of such knowledge, was enough to make human history a local film, useful only while certain directions remained mismatched.

Had an astronomer seen it on a celestial scale, perhaps he would have given it an acceptable name; in the mangrove, beneath mosquitoes and rotten cordage, the same principle seemed obscene. The final confirmation came when Faria, a heavy man devoted to simple facts, knelt to cut the cord. His knife touched the mud without sinking, and in that same instant all the dark windows of the hermitage lit from within with a colourless clarity, leaving the walls incomplete instead of illuminating them. Through the openings I saw the interior of the chapel repeated in successive sizes, each one slightly displaced, as though the building were only the visible margin of a construction placed outside our lengths. Faria did not scream then, as those who saw nothing later claimed; he looked at the windows with a kind of animal obedience. The knife fell from his hand and remained suspended a span above the mud, turning around an axis that passed through no place.

The clarity of the hermitage showed pews, altar, and cracks, and in each repetition those objects lost a simple quality, first weight, then width, then the very obligation to be before us. When I tried to call Faria, my voice returned thin and delayed from one of the smaller chapels that did not fit inside the first, and he no longer seemed to recognize my name. After that there are gaps I will not try to fill with defensive invention. I remember Faria's cry ceasing before it began, my hand closed over the soaked notebook, and the return to the quay at daybreak, when the workers found me lying among sacks of lime I could not have reached without passing by them. Faria was declared drowned, although the tide was low, and the company, eager to avoid expenses, accepted the conclusion with a speed that taught me something about administrative mercy.

Questioned by the doctor, I answered what any sane man would answer, and for that reason was considered feverish. I did not mention the steps in the shadow or the captain's interrupted silence, for neither of those facts could be presented without seeming metaphor. The notebook, however, dried in my room with its pages stuck together exactly at the passages Serrão had marked with nine lines. The police preferred this fever because it spared questions about the suspended knife, the intact cord, and the straight marks the sun dried before noon. There remain in my possession Serrão's notebook, my drawing of the markers, and a splinter of hardened mud that changes weight when the bell of any nearby church begins to vibrate. I will deliver these objects to whoever can interdict São Bento without announcing the reasons, for I know now that certain truths become invitations when drafted in public language.

If this account seems confused, attribute the failure to the man who still wakes hearing a tide without water; if it seems too clear, close it immediately and prevent anyone from seeking the grey strip at the next greater low tide. I have not returned to São Bento, and I ask that no one read cowardice where there is only a belated fidelity to the real. The company will resume dredging when the budget dispute ends, and there is already talk of students willing to photograph the strip at the next greater low tide. If I cannot prevent that gesture, at least I leave written that certain doors open outside the ideas of exterior and interior, toward a direction in which the word door is already a dangerous concession. I also ask that the splinter be destroyed if it begins to answer distant bells, for perhaps sound is only the poorest form by which that direction learns to seek us.

If any authority judges this warning exaggerated, let him visit the archive before the mangrove, for documents, at least, cannot pull the visitor by the shadow. I repeat: no experiment must precede total interdiction.
