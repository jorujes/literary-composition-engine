# The Colour of the Mud

I write this because, since the careless publication of the municipal report on the inlet of Sao Bento, three office men and a provincial physician have taken to repeating an explanation that may kill other curious men. Their version speaks of saline emanations, collective hysteria, and the deformation of old buoys; mine, which I kept back through prudence and shame, depends on notes taken before I myself accepted the danger. I do not ask complete belief, but only the suspension of that robust curiosity which sends young engineers down into the mud when the tide has receded too far. I add that it was not my temperament to seek notoriety through maritime terrors, and that my education, though limited, was enough to distinguish magnetic declination from harbour fantasy. I took with me receipts, dated sketches, and the copy of the order which now obliges me to speak. Those papers may outlive my voice, for since that week I stumble over certain words as if they had lost their natural direction. This precaution is all the more necessary because the order turns my silence into complicity, and because no one, reading administrative figures alone, will perceive the kind of invitation that a technical error may conceal.

I was called to the place in March, as a modest draughtsman of plans for a dredging company that intended to open a minor channel beside the mangrove. Sao Bento did not appear on tourist maps, having only a greened stone quay, two lime warehouses, and a closed hermitage whose bell had been removed without parish record. The first abnormal sign seemed ridiculous: all the compasses, including Captain Faria's pocket compass, erred by a few degrees only when pointed toward a band of grey mud among the roots. The people there had the dry reserve of settlements that have seen many fevers and few miracles, so that no one hurried to decorate my work with legends. Only an old woman, keeper of the keys to the hermitage, refused to cross the square when my instruments lay on the quay table. She said that metal awakened an old thing; then she fell silent, as if the sentence had betrayed her. Even the dogs, which slept beside the barrels, avoided the table when the compass was there, and that domestic detail, of no scientific value, returned later with an insistence that still wears me down.

I recorded that detail from professional habit, though the overseer laughed and attributed everything to the rust of the winches. Later I learned that the fishermen had avoided the band since the childhood of their grandfathers, not from fear of drowning, for the water there scarcely covered the ankles, but because any stake driven into that stretch reappeared at dawn twisted against the current. Local superstition called it the breathing of the bottom, a coarse name that seemed less foolish to me when I saw the mud move without bubbles. I examined the stake the next morning and found the wood sound, though its fibres opened in angles no lateral strain would have produced. The clay clinging to its point had dried in plates so regular that they seemed cut with a ruler. I kept one of them out of simple curiosity, and that curiosity, which then seemed to me a virtue, was the first moral failure of the whole affair. Each time I turned the plate of mud, its faces seemed to exchange orientation without altering form, a phenomenon too small for denunciation and too large for oblivion.

On the second afternoon I found, in the damp archive of the larger warehouse, an 1879 notebook belonging to a surveyor named Valerio Serrao. Its pages, eaten at the edges, described the same deviation of needles and added drawings of a net of parallel lines under the silt, lines which he swore he had measured by graduated rods before abandoning them. The last note spoke neither of monster nor apparition, and for that reason disturbed me more: it said only that the lines obeyed a tide that did not come from the sea. Serrao's name was absent from the official lists; two kerosene receipts and a letter of collection, however, proved that he had been there for six weeks. The tone of his notes passed gradually from technical impatience to an almost diseased concision, as if each sentence were written against an outside pressure. One drawing, more often repeated than the others, showed nine lines that never touched on the paper and yet were marked by him as one line seen from the wrong place. I do not know whether Serrao went mad, fled, or simply understood before us the uselessness of remaining where measurement itself had begun to serve another space.

I tried to confirm the notebook by ordinary means, for there still remained in me a considerable contempt for the tales of the quay. I buried three iron markers, made sketches to scale, and asked the telegraph boy to register the exact hour of each withdrawal of the water. By the third cycle the markers formed an impossible triangle, not because they moved much, but because they preserved constant distances among themselves while the whole orientation turned like a leaf caught in an invisible current. I do not expect such measurements to convince those who did not stand before them, for common language makes of every impossibility a copyist's trick. I can only state that I remade the marks with different instruments and that the result mocked them all equally. Faria, who had formerly derided my caution, fell silent when a bottle left among the markers rolled up the slight incline and stopped at the least low point. The telegraph boy, seeing my drawings, asked whether I had used wet paper, for he swore the lines drew nearer when no one breathed over the table.

It was then that I noticed the smell, if smell one may call a dry impression of heated stone inside the throat. None of the workers perceived it at the same instant as I did, and each, when separately questioned, indicated a different origin: the lime kiln, an extinguished candle in the hermitage, the hull of a boat that no longer existed. This divergence did not weaken my suspicion; rather it suggested that something was using ordinary memories to clothe a presence without quality of its own. Each man's expression changed according to the memory selected by the odour, and it was that variation which prevented me from accepting a simple chemical cause. The young telegraph operator grew pale while speaking of a hospital room; the caulker swore he heard wet wood; Faria made the sign of the cross without noticing it. None of this came from the mangrove as vapour, but from some joining between the mangrove and intimate deposits which ought not to have answered a tide. Since then I distrust the senses when they offer themselves as memory, for perhaps they are less windows of the soul than instruments available to faceless forces.

On the following night I agreed to accompany Faria to the uncovered band, taking lantern, cord, notebook, and a courage so theatrical that today it seems almost obscene. The moon made of the mangrove a writing of broken shadows, and the mud, which from a distance had seemed smooth, revealed a texture of tiny slanting blades, all turned toward a point with no reference in the sky. When the lantern touched that point on the ground, there was no gleam, noise, or opening; there was only the physical certainty that I was looking at the inner side of a surface larger than the earth. There was, however, a severe order in that appearance of disorder, and perhaps for that reason I continued when every prudence ordered me to withdraw. The blades did not shine; they received the light as numbers receive an operation, changing without visible transition. I felt, before formulating it, that we were not before an opening, but before the shallow edge of an operation that only by accident touched our senses. Faria noticed my trembling and smiled with pity, a common gesture which for one second made still more abominable the exact indifference of the mud before us.

Faria wished to retreat, but the cord that bound us to the quay began to weigh as if it hung vertically downward. I remember with shameful clarity that I tried to explain the fact by suction, by magnetism, by any word capable of keeping the scene within learned mechanics. Then the captain showed me the shadow of our legs projected in the direction opposite the light of the lantern, and in that shadow there was, for a moment, the form of steps that no body cast. Faria murmured an incomplete prayer, and the prayer seemed to prolong itself on the ground in small straight breaks. I heard no voice in answer, yet the steps we took backward produced, ahead of us, successive depressions in the intact mud. It was at that instant that I lost the last confidence in the notion of distance, for every retreat seemed only to deepen our participation in the point from which we fled. None of those depressions corresponded to the size of our feet, and all, when touched by the lantern, showed that same impossible tendency to turn inward.

I do not affirm that I saw inhabitants or intelligence, and it would be more honest to confess that the word intelligence still seems too human to me. What I saw was a rule at work: the blades of mud changed inclination according to beats coming from no depth, and each change brought into agreement the absent bell, Serrao's rods, and the error of the compasses. I understood that the place, instead of hiding a thing beneath the mangrove, exposed a fragment of correspondence between our matter and a system whose natural direction crossed us obliquely. I say rule for lack of a more decent term, but the word suggests intention where perhaps there was only compatibility. What surrounded us did not hate, call, or hunt; it adjusted. And that adjustment, perceived in a poor inlet by two men unworthy of such knowledge, was enough to make of human history a local film, useful only while certain directions remained misaligned. Had an astronomer seen it on a celestial scale, he might have given it an acceptable name; in the mangrove, beneath mosquitoes and rotten cordage, the same principle seemed obscene.

The final confirmation came when Faria, a heavy man and a devotee of simple facts, knelt to cut the cord. His knife touched the mud without sinking, and at the same instant all the dark windows of the hermitage lit from within with a colourless clarity, making the walls incomplete rather than illuminating them. Through the openings I saw the interior of the chapel repeated in successive sizes, each slightly displaced, as if the building were only the visible margin of a construction set outside our lengths. Faria did not scream then, as those who saw nothing later said; he looked at the windows with a kind of animal obedience. The knife fell from his hand and remained suspended a handbreadth above the mud, turning around an axis that passed through nowhere. The clarity of the hermitage showed benches, altar, and cracks, and in each repetition those objects lost a simple quality, first weight, then width, then the very obligation of being before us. When I tried to call Faria, my voice returned thin and delayed from one of the smaller chapels that could not fit inside the first, and he no longer seemed to recognize my name.

After that there are gaps which I shall not try to fill with defensive invention. I remember Faria's cry ceasing before it began, my hand closed upon the soaked notebook, and the return to the quay at dawn, when the workers found me lying among sacks of lime which I could not have reached without passing by them. Faria was reported drowned, though the tide was low, and the company, eager to avoid expenses, accepted the conclusion with a speed that taught me something about administrative mercy. Questioned by the doctor, I answered what any sane man would answer, and for that reason was considered feverish. I did not mention the steps in the shadow or the captain's interrupted silence, for none of those facts could be presented without seeming metaphor. The notebook, however, dried in my room with its pages stuck exactly at the passages Serrao had marked with nine lines. The police preferred that fever because it spared questions about the suspended knife, the intact cord, and the straight marks which the sun dried before noon.

There remain in my possession Serrao's notebook, my drawing of the markers, and a shard of hardened mud that changes weight when the bell of any nearby church begins to vibrate. I shall deliver these objects to anyone who can interdict Sao Bento without announcing the reasons, for I know now that certain truths become invitations when written in public language. If this account seems confused, attribute the failure to the man who still wakes hearing a tide without water; if it seems too clear, close it immediately and prevent anyone from seeking the grey band at the next greater ebb. I have not returned to Sao Bento, and I ask that no one read cowardice where there is only a late fidelity to the real. The company will resume the dredging when the dispute over funds ends, and there is already talk of students willing to photograph the band at the next greater ebb. If I cannot prevent that gesture, at least I leave written that certain doors open outside the ideas of exterior and interior, toward a direction in which the word door is already a dangerous concession. I ask also that the shard be destroyed if it begins to answer distant bells, for perhaps sound is only the poorest form by which that direction learns to seek us. If any authority judges this warning exaggerated, let him visit the archive before the mangrove, for documents, at least, cannot pull the visitor by the shadow. I repeat: no experiment must precede total interdiction.
